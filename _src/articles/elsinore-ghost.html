SECTION 1 · BOTTOM LINE

An unidentified man, 13 eliminations, and two open ends

The short version. Each point is built out in the sections that follow, and every FBI statement carries a Part / page / Bates citation.

1. The tip. Six days after the hijacking, a skydiving-magazine editor the FBI calls a reliable source reported that the Bureau's sketch looked like a stranger he had talked with at Skylark Field, Elsinore, California, in mid-to-late August 1971. The stranger asked how a person could jump out of a jet. The source told him it could be done from an aircraft with an inward-opening door or a rear stairwell, with full flaps and gear down for drag. The stranger also asked about jump centers around Seattle and Portland; the source named three, including one "just north of Portland" on the Washington side of the Columbia (64/333/26776; 64/389/26839).

2. The tipster. His name is redacted (b7D) in the Cooper Vault release. A December 14, 1971 Los Angeles teletype that survives unredacted in The Black Vault's FOIA file on Lyle Hazen Cameron (FOIPA #1358876-000, pp. 67–69) names the Skydiver editor-publisher at 15206 Raymond, Gardena as Lyle Cameron, reports no derogatory information on him, and notes his 1968 service as a Bureau-approved informant (PSI - R). With the 1970 USPA directory and the redaction widths, that identifies the tipster as Cameron (§4).

3. The sweep. SA Edward A. Romanoff and center staff went through about 12,000 registration cards, photographed 433, listed ~125 April–August 1971 registrants on 12/10/71, and matched the 433 cards against August 1971 flight logs on 12/14–15/71, leaving 31 names. The source and the center between them knew 16 of those men by sight (8 + 8 = 16), leaving 15 cards no one could put a face to (64/392/26842; 64/395/26845).

4. Fourteen of the 15 leads were run down; 13 are documented as closed. The 12/22/71 leads airtel (64/404–405/26854–55) assigned the 15 cards by location: 1 to New York at East Rockaway, 1 to Buffalo at Tonawanda, 1 to San Francisco at Los Gatos, 3 to the Santa Ana RA (2 in Santa Ana, 1 in Laguna Beach), and the rest—9 by arithmetic—to the LA case agent in the metropolitan area. Return memos in Parts 18, 20, 55, 56 and 64 close out 13 men. Two ends stay open in the release: the Los Gatos lead has no return at all, and the JPL Pasadena man's Utah alibi was sent to Salt Lake City for checking and no answer appears (56/21/23406) (§5).

5. The list. The 12/10/71 memo of ~125 April–August 1971 registrants (64/371–382/26821–32) left eight names unredacted: Blackmore, Booth, Chambers, Douglas, Mc Collon [McCollom], Nowell, Parry and Sanell. Only Guy M. Blackmore is confirmed among the 15 leads (64/385/26835). Redaction-width matches between the other seven and the 1972 interview memos are possibilities only, because any of the ~117 blacked-out names could fit the same boxes—and a check of the men's card addresses rules several of those matches out (§7, §8).

6. The ending. On 1/28/72 the source was shown photographs of the Grand Island, NY man and of two other men from the leads; none was the man he had talked to (64/387/26837; 64/406/26856). No document in the release says the Elsinore stranger was ever identified.

7. What is new in this report: background and credibility assessment of tipster Lyle Hazen Cameron from his 129-page FBI file (FOIPA #1358876- 000), plus the 1964 FBI telephone-toll trace (LA 44-895, p. 39) behind the Jack Ruby / Lee Harvey Oswald story (§4); recovery of four previously overlooked elimination memos —the East Rockaway NY caterer (20/212–214/6169–71) and the three Orange County jumpers (56/190–191/23591–92), including the Laguna Beach skydiver who told the FBI Cooper must have been a novice because an experienced jumper would have brought his own rig (§5); an address check showing that the card addresses of McCollom, Nowell and Douglas don't match the Orange County leads their redaction widths seemed to fit (§8); verification of the August 24, 1971 Look magazine jet-jump advertisement cited by Cameron (64/394/26844): a U.S. Army Airborne spread showing a paratrooper jumping from a C-141 jet (§3); plus enlarged snippets of all eight unredacted men on the 12/10/71 list (§7) and a 1992 death record for John Loftin Nowell Jr. (Appendix A6).

BOTTOM-LINE VERDICT

The August 1971 Elsinore conversation is one of the most specific pre-hijacking leads in the Norjak file, reported by an experienced, FBI-vetted skydiving figure (Lyle Hazen Cameron). The FBI's card sweep ran down 14 of the 15 August flight-log leads and documents 13 eliminations, but the stranger in the military Cochrane jump boots was never identified. The open FOIA targets are the 15th lead (Los Gatos, CA, 64/405), the Salt Lake City return on the JPL man (56/21), and the 433 card photographs (LA 164-497-1B1).

SECTION 2

The place: Skylark Field, Elsinore

Southern California's busiest jump center in 1971, about 60 miles southeast of Los Angeles on the shore of Lake Elsinore.

The field. According to Skydive World's "Project Pi" history, Cy Perkins and his brother bought Gordon Hunt Airport in 1952 and renamed it Skylark Field. Skylark Aviation began running a drop zone there in 1959. At its peak the fleet was two Howards, a Twin Beech called the "Elsinore Queen" and a Cessna 180 called the "Yellow Jacket". (Outside source: projectpi.skydiveworld.com/DZelsinr.htm.) Who ran it. In the FBI's paperwork the jump operation is Elsinore Paricenter, Inc., Route 2, Box 501, Elsinore (64/371/26821). The 1970 USPA directory in the file lists the drop zone's area safety officer as Larry L. Perkins at the same address, and lists Skylark Aviation there as an FAA-certificated parachute loft (54/278–279/22743–44). Volume. The field logged 29,110 jumps in 1970 and was on course for more than 30,000 in 1971 (64/395/26845). The source estimated 8,000–10,000 jumpers had come through since June 1971, most of them repeat jumpers who might make six to eight jumps in a day (64/390–391/26840–41). Registration cards. Every jumper filled out a 5×7 card before his first jump at Elsinore, however experienced he was. Later visits produced only an unnamed ticket receipt. A USPA licence number, if the jumper had one, was added to his original card (64/332/26775; 64/390/26840). Cards from before 1969 recorded no height (64/392/26842). Which FAA office covered it. The same 1970 directory lists the Southern California General Aviation District Offices. The only one in the Inland Empire is Ontario, at Ontario International Airport (54/279/22744). That office moved to Riverside in 1979 (Sanell SF-50, §7). [inference] Elsinore, in Riverside County, almost certainly fell in the Ontario GADO's district in 1971, including oversight of Skylark Aviation's aircraft and certificated loft.

The conversation (FD-302, 12/1/71) and the 12/16/71 Air Force addendum

The man "asked several unusual questions, such as how would one go about jumping out of jets." The source told him "it could be done if the door of the aircraft opened inward or if it had a rear stairwell that could be raised and lowered," and that "full flaps and lowered landing gear would supply the drag needed to exit from a jet" (64/389/26839). The man asked where jump centers were in the Northwest (the 11/30 tip memo says Seattle and Portland, 64/333/26776). The source named three in Washington: "one by the Boeing Air Field in Seattle, one on the Washington side of the Columbia River, just north of Portland, Oregon, and the other center inbetween." The man did not write any of them down. They talked about jump altitudes; the source answered in terms of record altitudes, oxygen and free-fall time, and later added (12/16/71) that the 10,000-foot level is "reminiscent of Air Force training"—the maximum altitude considered safe without oxygen in AFP 160-10-3 ("Your Body in Flight") —and that a man with Air Force experience might fit (64/404/26854). Flight 305 flew below 10,000 ft at the hijacker's demand. The source remembered him "rather vividly" for his Cochrane boots, which he said are peculiar to military jumpers and are not worn by teams, clubs, sport jumpers or novices, and noted that "nobody else has ever purposed such questions to him" (64/389–390/26839–40).

Caveats the record carries

The Raleigh detail came after the publicity. The source said he "recalled this while studying the Government memorandum containing the description" of the hijacker (64/393/26843), and the Louisville Premium Center had no record of parachute requests (56/189/23590). He offered his own innocent explanation—now verified: on 12/9/71 (64/394/26844), Cameron noted that pages 46–47 of the August 24, 1971 issue of Look magazine carried an advertisement showing a parachutist jumping out of a jet aircraft, which "may have stimulated questions" by someone who saw it. Located and reproduced below, that two-page spread is a U.S. Army Airborne recruiting ad ("When you jump, it's just you." / "Today's Army wants to join you.", code 251 23-8-71) depicting a four-panel sequence of a lone paratrooper exiting and descending beneath a four-engine C-141 Starlifter military jet transport. Complexion and build conflict: "light" and "slim" vs. the crew's "olive" and "average to well built". The date shifted from "July or August" (12/1) to mid-to-late August (12/9).

In short. The technical overlap between the August 1971 Elsinore conversation and the November 24, 1971 hijacking is striking: a rear stairwell exit, full flaps and lowered landing gear for drag, a 10,000-foot unpressurized ceiling, and Northwest jump centers including one just north of Portland. At the same time, the physical description shows real tension (light complexion and slim build vs. the crew's olive complexion and average-to-well-built frame), the Raleigh coupon detail surfaced only after the witness read the FBI flyer, and the witness himself noted that an August 1971 Look magazine ad could have sparked the questions.

SECTION 4

The tipster: Lyle Hazen Cameron

The Cooper Vault release withholds the tipster's name under exemption b7D. Cross-checking the Cooper file against the 1970 USPA directory and The Black Vault's 129-page FBI FOIA release on Lyle Hazen Cameron (FOIPA #1358876-000, released 8/30/2018) identifies the tipster as Cameron with high confidence: the publisher's Gardena address, the USPA directory, the redaction widths and the "reliable source" history all point to him.

Who Lyle Cameron was in the world of parachuting—and his role at Elsinore

In 1971, Lyle Hazen Cameron (born January 2, 1931, Union, Maine; died June 19, 1993, aged 62, piloting a Cessna 180 out of Trujillo, Honduras) was one of the most prominent and connected figures in American sport parachuting. He was the founder, editor, and publisher of Skydiver magazine—the sport's premier independent trade monthly—and had served on the national board of the Parachute Club of America / United States Parachute Association (USPA) since 1961, including terms as Chairman of the Executive Board and National Director (80/362/35261; Cameron FOIA p. 32). Internationally, he served as coach, team leader, and world-championship judge for the U.S. Parachute Team across Europe and the Soviet bloc (Cameron FOIA pp. 18, 32, 38).

His position at the Elsinore jump center: Although some later forum accounts mistakenly called Cameron the drop-zone operator (which was the Perkins family's Skylark Aviation / Elsinore Paricenter), his FBI file documents his exact relationship to Skylark Field: Cameron was an independent commercial pilot, free-fall aerial photographer for film and television, and senior skydiver whose "general area of operations is the Elsinore, California airport" where "he spends most of his time" (Cameron FOIA pp. 16, 18, 27). He had been an Elsinore fixture since making the drop zone's first double baton pass in January 1960, knew the staff and regulars by sight, and was trusted by the center to screen the August 1971 suspect list alongside SA Romanoff (64/395/26845).

VERDICT ON THE SOURCE: LYLE CAMERON'S CREDIBILITY

Cameron was an experienced, well-connected and FBI-vetted source. He was a seven-year LAPD officer (1955– 62), a reserve officer, a 1968 Bureau security informant, and a USPA National Director who spent most of his time at Skylark Field. LA's 1971 background check found nothing derogatory; a 1968 referral over surplus Navy gear ended with the AUSA declining prosecution for lack of criminal intent. No motive to invent the conversation is known. As Skydiver 's publisher he did offer to print the FBI sketch—worth noting, though it is not evidence of embellishment. The weak points are ordinary memory problems after the publicity: the Raleigh coupons surfaced only after he read the FBI description, and the date moved from "July or August" to mid-to-late August. His core account—a fortyish stranger in military Cochrane boots asking how to get out of a jet and where the Northwest jump centers were—comes from a credible witness, but it rests on his memory alone.

VERDICT ON THE FBI'S CARD SWEEP

Contrary to the common assumption that the FBI left the 15 Elsinore cards hanging, Parts 18, 20, 55, 56 and 64 show that agents ran down 14 of the 15 leads between December 1971 and March 1972 and documented 13 eliminations by alibi, records or appearance. Two ends stay open in the release: the Los Gatos, California lead (64/405/26855) has no return serial, and the Salt Lake City check of the JPL man's Utah alibi (56/21/23406) never comes back. Some eliminations are thinner than others: the Grand Island man's alibi rested on a co-worker's memory, though Cameron also rejected his photographs.

Don Allen McCary, 1991: "the pilot is Cooper"

On 11/29/91, during the 20th-anniversary coverage, Don Allen McCary walked into the Riverside RA. A deaf skydiver from Riverside (born 10/5/41, 5′7′′, 135 lb), he brought a photo of himself in jump gear at Skylark Field and stated "the pilot was identical to D. B. We assume the man McCary is referring to is the man seated to McCary's right (viewer's left) inside the aircraft doorway with his booted foot sticking out. LA forwarded the photo and log to Seattle as 1A(827) (106/148/48444; 89/23–31/39410–18).

What McCary's log shows (89/27–31):

Jumps 31–50, the span McCary gave for the photo, run from 10/3/70 to 9/17/72. His last Elsinore jumps before the hijacking were on 7/17/71, from a Howard and a Cessna; he logged no jumps in August 1971 (his next entry is "11/?/71" at Perris Valley). The aircraft tail number in the photo ends in "...810", which does not match current FAA registry entries for Larry L. Perkins or Skylark Aviation.

December 1971, Latin Sky Diving Club (54/343/22810; 53/449–450/22308–09; 98/275/44343): A Los Angeles teletype and report note a local club of 10–20 East LA skydivers who jumped at Perris and Elsinore; the named subject was eliminated after an LAPD/NCIC check and composite comparison. Similarly, the 1971–1986 secondary tips involve men whose ages, heights, or backgrounds were quickly ruled out.

OVERALL VERDICT: PREMONITION OR BOOO-SHEET?

The Elsinore conversation is not "booo-sheet": it comes from an experienced, FBI-vetted skydiving figure (Lyle Hazen Cameron) with no known motive to invent it, who described a stranger in military Cochrane boots asking how to get out of a jet and where the Seattle and Portland jump centers were, three months before Flight 305. But it is one man's memory, sharpened after the publicity, and the questions also fit an ordinary jumper who had just seen the Look ad. The FBI ran down 14 of the 15 card leads and documents 13 eliminations, and Cameron didn't recognize the photographs he was shown. That leaves three possibilities: the stranger was the Los Gatos lead (64/405) or the JPL man whose Utah alibi check never came back (56/21); his visit never produced a matching post-1969 card and August surname; or he was an innocent jumper.
