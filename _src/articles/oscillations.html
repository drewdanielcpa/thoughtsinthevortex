============ SUMMARY ============
Summary

Key findings

The short version. Each point is built out in the sections that follow, with citations to the FBI Vault files as Part / PDF page / Bates.

  1. Cooper jumped at 8:11 PM, give or take a minute. The bump hit while the co-pilot was on the company radio. NWA's Minneapolis office logged it live (2210 Central time), and the 8:12 teletype reads "305 GETTING SOME OSCLLTNS IN THE CABIN." The January 1972 sled test reproduced the bump, and NWA fixed the time at 0411 GMT ±1 minute.
  2. The plane was over rural north Clark County. Radar puts it near Highland and La Center, just south of the Lewis River, about 10 nm north of the Portland VORTAC (which actually sat at Battle Ground). It was at 10,000 ft and 170 knots indicated, with the gear down and flaps at 15°.
  3. The "later jump" theory is possible but not likely. It holds that 8:11 was only Cooper's weight going onto the stairs and that he jumped a few minutes later, during an 8:12–8:20 gap in NWA's contact with the crew. Nothing in the files rules that out. But every crew account describes one event, the 8:11 reaction was sharp and physical, and when contact resumed at 8:22 the crew couldn't say whether he was still aboard. The FBI and NWA, with the crew, the tape and the radar in hand, settled on 8:11.
  4. Cooper knew roughly where he was, not precisely. He knew local geography, and dead reckoning from his own speed demands would have put him within about 7 nm (8 statute miles) along V-23, the route his demands all but forced. But he couldn't see heading, ground speed or wind, there was cloud below him, and the FBI concluded he jumped as soon as he practically could.
  5. The FBI/NWA 1972 drop zone holds up. Rebuilt from scratch with the real 1971 weather-balloon winds and the radar position, every 8:11 landing zone, started from the radar track, falls inside NWA's January 1972 envelope. The radar timing could be a minute off, so the 8:12 zones count equally; about 70% of those fall inside too.
  6. V-23 or bust. Low, slow and gear-down left one practical route south, Victor 23 past Portland, and Reno was about as far as it could go. Someone who knew aviation could have expected that and timed his jump by the clock.
  7. What stays open: radar precision and timing, low-level winds, steering under the canopy, search coverage, a Continental pilot's discredited 65-kt wind, and the Tena Bar money (a later installment).

"DME" is distance in nautical miles from a radio navigation station. The "Portland VORTAC" (116.6 MHz) the crew refers to was located at Battle Ground, Washington, about 15 miles north of downtown Portland. V-23 (Victor 23) is the federal airway the plane flew south from Seattle. KIAS (knots indicated airspeed) is what the cockpit gauge reads; in the thin air at 10,000 ft it reads well below the true airspeed (TAS), so 170 KIAS was about 195 kt true (about 224 mph). TAT (total air temperature) reads warmer than the outside air because the air heats up as it hits the probe at speed; the crew's −7°C means about −12°C outside. ARINC (Aeronautical Radio, Inc.) ran the radio-telephone relay airlines used to patch calls through.

Section 2

When did he jump?

Four records made at the time, plus a test flight seven weeks later, all point to the same minute.

The test flight

On January 6, 1972, NWA and the FBI flew the same 727 in the same configuration and pushed a 235-lb sled down the aft stairs. It reproduced the pressure bump the crew had felt. NWA's memo of January 9 states that the flight tests "confirmed that the pressure fluctuation almost certainly occurred at the time the HJ left the airplane" 18 / 317 / 5650 · 19 / 16–17 / 5852–53 · 41 / 36–37 / 11404–05 · 110 / 267 / 566. NWA's earlier December 4, 1971 plot had put the bump at 0410 GMT and said the crew were "certain it occurred before 0415" 64 / 299 / 27165.

Copycat bailouts

The same signature showed up when others went out the 727's aft stairs. Himmelsbach told the press in 1976 that "the same rapid fluctuations in air pressure were noted in three bailouts from 727 airliners in the next few months," before the FAA ordered the rear doors locked 53 / 245 / 22583 (press). Outside accounts describe the two best-known cases. Martin McNally hijacked American Flight 119 out of St. Louis on June 23, 1972, and jumped over Indiana early on June 24; the remaining crew "felt their ears pop" as the cabin pressure fluctuated (Detroit Metro Times, 2017). Richard McCoy Jr. hijacked United Flight 855 on April 7, 1972, and jumped over Utah. In his case the crew didn't know he was gone until, past the last Utah waypoint, a crew member left the cockpit to check and found the cabin empty (Fear of Landing, 2018; Parachutist, 2011). Both are outside accounts, not FBI records; see Appendix A.

"Not yet reached Portland proper … definitely in the suburbs"

The crewman's interview places the plane "in the suburbs or immediate vicinity" of Portland 110 / 66 / 356. Read as geography, that sounds later than 8:11: at 8:11 the plane was about 27 miles north of downtown Portland. Read the way a pilot would, it fits. The crew were flying toward the "Portland" VORTAC, which was at Battle Ground, about 10 nm ahead, with the DME counting down inside the Portland terminal area. They were above a solid overcast, and the same interview says it "had not occurred to them" to pin down their exact position; they gave a window of 8:05 plus "5 to 10 minutes" and deferred to the company log for the exact time.

Verdict: the bump was Cooper leaving the plane

The pressure bump is what a 727 does when weight goes off the end of the aft stairs. The January 1972 sled test, in the same plane and the same configuration, reproduced it, and NWA concluded the fluctuation "almost certainly occurred at the time the HJ left the airplane." The copycat bailouts from 727s that followed showed the same signature. Whenever the bump happened, that is when he jumped.

============ 3 TWO EVENTS ============
Section 3

The "later jump" theory

A recurring community theory: the 8:11 oscillation was real but minor, Cooper's weight going onto the stairs rather than his exit. He actually jumped a few minutes later, during an 8:12–8:20 gap when NWA had no contact with the crew. Because NWA and the FBI had no record of that second event, they folded it into 8:11 when they fixed the jump time and drop zone.

So 8:11 itself is covered by a taped company channel and a live Minneapolis log. The gap is just after it: NWA logs nothing between the 8:12 teletype and 8:20, and the FAA transcript has only routine traffic from 8:13 to 8:16. Two FOIA targets (FBI, Seattle file 164-81) would test the theory: the NWA tape, in case it ran past 8:12, and any ARINC Seattle recording or log of 131.8 for 8:12–8:22.

For the theory

  • NWA's 8:12–8:20 gap. The teletype logs an exchange every one to eight minutes from 7:42 to 8:12, then nothing until 8:20. By then the crew is "already on" ARINC's 131.8, and Minneapolis's patch went up at 8:22 110 / 104–108 / 396–400 · 64 / 306 / 27172. A second event reported to the ARINC operator in that window could miss every NWA record.
  • NWA's first reading of 8:11 was "weight on stairs." Minneapolis logged the 8:10 report as "HJ weight now on stairs, stairs extended further" 64 / 305 / 27171, and the 8:12 teletype says he "must be doing something with air stairs." "After unsub departed" is the FBI's later reading 41 / 32 / 11400.
  • The crewman's timing. "5 to 10 minutes after … 8:05," then the crew "telephoned the company in Minneapolis shortly thereafter" 110 / 66 / 356. That fits a bump around 8:15 reported on the 8:22 patch as well as it fits 8:11.
  • The crewman's "suburbs of Portland" sounds later than 8:11 if read literally.
  • Tena Bar. If the $5,800 reached Tena Bar by natural means, no 8:11 or 8:12 zone can explain it. Every one of them (the FBI's and ours) drains to the Lewis River system: the Lewis, Cedar Creek and the East Fork. The Lewis joins the Columbia about 10 mi downstream of Tena Bar 43 / 377 / 17613. A jump during the gap, nearer the Columbia at Vancouver (about 8:17–8:18 at the flight's ground speed), could put money upstream of the bar. The exceptions are explanations that don't need a later jump: river dredging, or a person carrying the money there (Section 9).

Against

  • One event in every crew account. No interview describes a lesser jolt and then a bigger one. The crewman describes a single oscillation the crew "heard and felt" 110 / 66 / 356, "reported by radio … at time" 10 / 343 / 1570.
  • The 8:11 reaction was sharp. The co-pilot breaks off mid-sentence and turns to the gauge "because of an increase in cabin pressure," pulling out his earplug 41 / 32 / 11400. An increase is the signature of the stairs snapping closed; more weight pushing them out should lower cabin pressure. The "increase" wording is the FBI's, not the co-pilot's.
  • The first words on the patch don't report a jump. At 8:22 the crew said the aft cabin lights were off and they "could not see whether HJ was still aboard" 64 / 306 / 27172. Seattle's 8:20 teletype adds nothing new either, though NWA Seattle "had been monitoring all communications with flight."
  • Routine talk with Seattle Center at 8:13–8:16. A check-in and an altimeter setting, nothing about the hijacker 36 / 18 / 6241. This is weak: they didn't mention 8:11 to Center either.
  • The people who could check, did. NWA's determination used the company tape, the flight recorder, the crew and McChord radar 41 / 37 / 11405 · 110 / 267 / 566. With the crew available, they fixed 0411 GMT, reproduced the bump with the sled, and kept it through the 1973 re-plot.
  • "Suburbs" fits 8:11 once "Portland" means the Portland VORTAC at Battle Ground (Section 2).
Verdict: possible, but not likely; the jump was at 8:11 PM, ±1 minute

Nothing in the files rules out a second, unrecorded event during NWA's 8:12–8:20 gap, and NWA's own first reading of 8:11 ("weight now on stairs") leaves room for one. But every crew account describes one event, the 8:11 reaction was sharp and physical, and the crew's first words when the patch came up at 8:22 were that they couldn't tell whether he was still aboard. NWA and the FBI, with the crew, the company tape and the radar in hand, fixed 0411 GMT and confirmed it with the sled test. The theory now rests on a gap rather than a missing call. An ARINC Seattle record of 131.8 for 8:12–8:22, or NWA's tape if it ran past 8:12, would settle it.

============ 4 DID HE KNOW ============
Section 4

Did he know where he was? Criminal Mastermind or Dumb Luck

If Cooper picked his spot, 8:11 means something more than "as soon as he could." The record supports a general sense of position, not a precise one.

Criminal Mastermind

  • He showed general geographic awareness earlier in the flight.
    • He told the stewardess it was "only about twenty minutes from McChord to the Seattle-Tacoma airport," which was accurate 26 / 473 / 8883.
    • In the holding pattern he looked out and said "We are over Tacoma now," with no announcement prompting it. The FBI took this as a sign he had lived in the Northwest 26 / 473 / 8883 · 41 / 16 / 11384.
  • His demands (10,000 ft, no more than 170 kt, gear down, flaps 15°) made the plane slow and its timing predictable. It's reasonable to believe he could have had a loose idea of his location based on the time that had passed.
  • His demands all but forced the plane onto V-23, the standard low-altitude route south past Portland (Section 5).
  • The FBI itself noted the crew "could see the lights of Portland and other distinctive lights in that area, so given knowledge of the specific area the hijacker could very easily have made a jump to a specific location" 18 / 143 / 5476.
  • Dead reckoning (our calculation). Using only what he knew (the 170 kt he demanded, taken as his ground speed, no wind and the 7:36 takeoff), Cooper may have thought he had flown about 99 nm by 8:11. On V-23 that is about 7 nm (8 statute miles) south of where radar had him, near Battle Ground, so if he was timing his jump he would have believed he was even closer to Portland. (170 kt indicated is really about 195 kt true at 10,000 ft, but nothing in the cabin told him that.)
  • He was ready early, then waited. About four minutes after takeoff (about 7:40; NWA's log has 7:41) he sent the stewardess forward; she last saw him in the aisle with a nylon cord tied around his waist 110 / 18–19 / 308–09 · 10 / 428 / 1661. He had already wrapped the money bag in parachute cord, with a loop for a handle, while the plane was still on the ground 110 / 54 / 344. He called the cockpit at 7:42 about trouble getting the stairs down, and the stair light came on soon after 10 / 428 / 1661 · 110 / 254 / 551. Yet he didn't go until 8:11, roughly 25–30 minutes later, which suggests he may have been waiting for something.
  • He jumped close to where he started. He boarded at Portland International Airport, and Point A is only about 22 miles north of it.

Dumb Luck

  • The pilot chose V-23 "without consulting the hijacker" 54 / 75 / 23001 (press). From the cabin Cooper couldn't see heading, ground speed or wind, and nothing told him about a 33-kt headwind.
  • Off V-23 the same clock-based guess misses by 20–120 miles, on a straight line to Reno or Sacramento, the coast or east of the Cascades. He could only count on the route if he understood his demands (Section 5).
  • The ground was hidden under a solid overcast (cloud reports below). Himmelsbach: the plane was "on instruments in the higher of two layers of clouds all the way from Seattle to the vicinity of La Center" 53 / 245 / 22583 (press).
  • He wanted the stairs down for takeoff 64 / 153 / 26967, and the FBI concluded he jumped as soon as practical, delayed by the stairs and by tying on the money bag (below).
  • He didn't know how to work the airstair, the one function the stewardess said he seemed unfamiliar with 26 / 473 / 8883. This could suggest he wasn't well planned.
  • Himmelsbach, 1976: "Cooper could not have known where he was when he jumped" 53 / 245 / 22583 (press). …… Ralph, Ralph, Ralph.

Did he mean to jump near Seattle?

A community theory holds that Cooper planned to go out soon after takeoff, near Seattle, but the stairs held him up, and by the time they were down he judged it too late or the ground too wooded. NWA's log puts the plane here when the stairs came into play 64 / 304 / 27170:

  • He wanted out early. He asked for the stairs to be fully down for takeoff, then partly down, and was told neither was possible 64 / 304 / 27170 · 64 / 153 / 26967. By about 7:40 he had the cord tied around his waist 110 / 18–19 / 308–09. The FBI's own summary says he "probably wanted to jump from the aircraft as soon as possible after the takeoff" 41 / 26 / 11394.
  • The stairs did cost him a few minutes. At 7:42 he called the cockpit with "difficulty getting steps down"; at 7:43 the stair light came on, "stairs apparently now partially extended," with the plane 19 DME south of the Seattle VOR at 7,000 ft 64 / 304 / 27170.
  • At 7:43 he was still close to Seattle. About 22 mi south of Sea-Tac and 33 mi from downtown Seattle, 10 mi south of Tacoma, between Spanaway and Frederickson, about 5 mi east of McChord AFB and next to Fort Lewis.
  • But he then waited 28 minutes. With the stairs already partly open, the plane flew on over the wooded foothills between Roy and Eatonville, past Mossyrock and the Cowlitz River, and over the hills 15–20 mi east of Castle Rock and Kelso. The Spanaway–Frederickson prairie at 7:43 was some of the most open ground on the whole route, and he finally jumped over the timbered hills of north Clark County. "Too wooded" doesn't explain waiting for more of the same.

Our read: the stairs explain a few minutes of delay, not 28. If he had meant to go near Seattle, he could have gone at about 7:43–7:50, still within about 30 mi of Sea-Tac. Something else set 8:11: the climb to 10,000 ft (7:51–7:53), the money bag, nerve, or a target further south.

Could he see the lights of Portland?

A related theory says he jumped when Portland's lights first came into view. We checked the geometry and that night's airport cloud reports:

  • Distance isn't the limit. From 10,000 ft the horizon is about 140 miles away. In clear air, a city's lights carry 30–90 miles and a single streetlight 5–18 miles (Allard's law, using that night's 7–15 mile visibility). Portland and Vancouver would have been visible by about 7:50–8:00, and the Longview–Kelso mill lights passed close alongside around 8:05. "First sight" would not single out 8:11.
  • The cloud is the limit. At 8 PM Portland reported overcast at 5,000 ft, Troutdale at 4,000 and Toledo at 3,000; the Weather Bureau called it "overcast all the way from Woodland, Washington, to Eugene" 83 / 6 / 35327 · 110 / 248 / 540. Individual lights would show only through breaks; a diffuse glow on the deck is plausible.
  • One version fits. If the plane came out of the upper layer near La Center (Himmelsbach's "to the vicinity of La Center") and the crew saw Portland's glow ahead (the 12/23/71 memo), that would line up with 8:11. Nothing in the data confirms or rules it out.
  • His view was poor anyway. On the aft stairs he faced back and down. Portland was ahead, 4–8° below the horizon, into a roughly 195-kt slipstream with the gear down. The aft cabin lights were off, so his eyes would have been dark-adapted 64 / 306 / 27172.
Verdict: general awareness, not precise knowledge

He knew he was heading south somewhere near Portland. With a watch, and knowing his demands would keep the plane on V-23 (Section 5), he could have been within about 7 nm (8 statute miles). He did not know enough to hit a chosen spot. The FBI's reading, that he jumped as soon as he practically could, explains 8:11 without needing a target.

============ 5 V-23 ============
Section 5

V-23 or bust

Cooper never named a route. His demands picked one for him.

His terms, as the stewardess relayed them to the cockpitPart 110 · p. 54 · Bates 344

His terms were 10,000 ft or lower, about 170 kt, gear down, flaps 15° and the aft door open. Each one narrowed the way south:

  • Altitude. At 10,000 ft or lower the jet routes, which start at 18,000 ft, were out, and so was crossing the Cascades in cloud. Even on V-23 the crew needed 11,000 ft for terrain clearance from Red Bluff to Reno 64 / 306 / 27172.
  • Range. Minneapolis told the crew a non-stop to Mexico City was "impossible" with the gear down and flaps at 15°. Phoenix "might be within range but [Reno] would be better as far as range was concerned," and Cooper took Reno "without much, if any, objection" 64 / 304 / 27170. Even the Mexico plan ran through Yuma and Mazatlán 64 / 308 / 27174.
  • The route the crew got. A clearance "for Victor 23 at 10,000 feet," which they called "a standard low altitude routing" 64 / 322 / 27188. The captain picked it "without consulting the hijacker" 54 / 75 / 23001 (press).
NWA Minneapolis log: his configuration and the range problemPart 64 · p. 304 · Bates 27170

The other ways to Reno (our calculation)

Verdict: V-23 or bust

Someone who knew how airliners worked didn't need to ask about the route. Staying low, slow and gear-down left one practical way south, V-23 down the I-5 corridor past Portland, and Reno was about as far as the plane could get on it. Add his speed demand and the clock alone puts him within about 8 miles at 8:11. That doesn't prove he planned it: he never asked about the route, and he misjudged the range to Mexico and how the airstair worked. But a jump timed off V-23 fits where he went out.

The airway

V-23 ran from the Seattle VORTAC to the Battle Ground VORTAC: 186.6° true, which is 166° magnetic at the local variation of about 21° east. That matches the Continental pilot's "course of approximately 166 degrees" along V-23 39 / 227 / 15412 and the crew's "19 DME S SEA VOR on V23" 64 / 304 / 27170. The FBI radar tracks run 183–185° true, close to parallel, but at Point A the radar track sits about 2.2 mi west of the airway centerline. Section 8 starts its zones from the radar track, since radar is a measured position and the airway is only the intended course.

Ground-speed check

Taking the crew's fixes and the radar point, distance over time gives the ground speed, and the gap from true airspeed gives the headwind: about 33 kt overall. From 7:43 to 8:52 the plane covered about 184 nm in 69 minutes, an average of 160 kt. Over the 11 minutes from Point A to the 8:22 fix it averaged about 178 kt, matching the crew's own estimate of "205 miles per hour" (178 kt) 110 / 65 / 355. Point A's position and time fit the rest of the flight.

============ 9 RECALC ============
Section 8

Recalculated landing zones

A drift model run with the real 1971 winds, three canopy sizes, three ways of pulling, and two jump minutes. This is our calculation, not an FBI determination.

Method

  • Landing point = exit point + forward throw + wind drift in free fall + wind drift under canopy, stepped through altitude.
  • Free fall: about 10 s for the first 1,000 ft, then about 5.5 s per 1,000 ft (USPA spotting rule of thumb).
  • Canopy descent: from the FBI file, a 28-ft canopy falls about 17–18 ft/s with a 180–200 lb man, and 26- and 28-ft rigs were set to the same rate 20 / 71 / 6028. Scaled to about 230 lb and checked against the drag of flat circular canopies (Knacke). Sea-level rates: 24 ft 22.5–25, 26 ft 19.5–22, 28 ft 19–21 ft/s, faster in thinner air.
  • Pulls: quick = opens 3–5 s after exit; late = opens at 2,000–3,500 ft (NWA's lowest likely opening was 2,500 ft 110 / 268 / 567); none = free fall to the ground, with up to 2,600 ft of forward carry (NWA).
  • Exit: NWA's radar Point A at 8:11, within its ±0.5 nm, ±30 s; for 8:12, one minute further down the radar track (185° true) at the measured 160 kt.
  • Winds: the 1971 weather balloons at Salem and Quillayute (NOAA IGRA), blended to 8:11 PM: about 231° at 33 kt at 10,000 ft, easing to about 250° at 10 kt at the ground. That matches the flight's own timing (about a 33-kt headwind, 7:43–8:52) and the Portland meteorologist's winds aloft for the FBI 20 / 240–241 / 6197–98.
  • Uncertainty: 3,000 runs per case, with the wind profile rotated ±10° and scaled ±15%, and ground elevation 300–1,300 ft.

Give the 8:12 zones equal weight

NWA fixed the jump at 0411 GMT by matching the company tape, the Minneapolis log and the flight recorder to the radar, and allowed ±1 minute for it 110 / 267 / 566. A 2024 reading of the Air Force radar map (Cunningham's time labels, used by Humes et al.) runs about one minute ahead of NWA's, putting the plane at 8:11 where NWA has it at 8:12. We can't check that re-timing against the files, but a one-minute clock or plotting difference is entirely plausible for 1971. How it compares with the FBI zone

  • Inside NWA's envelope: 100% of the 8:11 zones, about 70% of the 8:12 zones, and about 82% of the two combined. Inside the 1972 Army search area: about 71%, 57% and 60%.

============ 10 OPEN ============
Section 9

Open questions and limits

  • Radar precision. NWA worked to ±0.5 nm, and the 1973 re-plot moved Point A about 0.9 mi. The raw McChord radar data is not in the released files, so we rely on NWA's plot of it. Timing is the other half: a one-minute difference in how the radar points are time-stamped moves every zone about 3 mi along the track (see Section 8).
  • Low-level winds. The soundings were taken four hours before the jump and 40–120 mi away, and Portland's surface wind swung from 130° to 270° between 7 and 8 PM. This matters most for a quick pull, with 6–7 minutes under canopy.
  • Steering. The model assumes he didn't steer. Slipping or steering, even crudely, for 6–7 minutes could move a quick-pull landing by about a mile.
  • Descent rate. If the canopy came down as slowly as the FBI's consulting expert implied (about 10 minutes under canopy) 14 / 67–69 / 3376–78, quick-pull drift grows from about 2.8 mi to about 3.8 mi with the real winds (about 3.4 mi at NWA's implied 17.5 ft/s).
  • The Continental pilot's 65-kt wind. In 1977 a Continental captain who flew Seattle–Portland that night told the FBI the wind at 10,000 ft was about 65 kt from 160–170°, which would have moved the zone 5–7 miles north 39 / 227–228 / 15412–13. His testimony has been shot down by the FBI, Flight 305's own data and independent weather data. The FBI closed the matter after a follow-up 39 / 331 / 15519; the flight's logged times show about a 33-kt headwind, and a 65-kt wind would have left the plane about 16 miles short of the radar at 8:11; and the 1971 weather balloons and the Portland meteorologist's winds aloft show about 20–35 kt from the southwest 20 / 240–241 / 6197–98.
  • A controller's "bump" call (new, 2026). In September 2026 Bruce Kinsler, a trainee controller at McChord in 1971, told Eric Ulis he heard the crew broadcast "in the blind": "We just felt a bump and we think, you know, he's jumped." He said he circled the plane on his scope "east-southeast of Centralia-Chehalis Airport" (as quoted by Edwards). The call could be real. At 7:59:15 Seattle Center sent the crew to 133.9, a frequency Center couldn't hear from them at that altitude, and Center has nothing until 8:13:14 on 120.9 36 / 17–18 / 6240–41. Why we doubt his time and place:
    • East-southeast of Chehalis meets V-23 at about 7:56–7:58 at the flight's ground speed, before the switch to 133.9.
    • NWA's live log has no bump before the 8:10 oscillation report, and no crew account mentions an earlier one 64 / 305 / 27171.
    • It is one witness, telling it for the first time 55 years later.
    Our read: a blind call right after the 8:11 bump, before the crew gave up on 133.9 and checked in on 120.9 at 8:13, with the remembered position off. An alternative: around 8:00 Cooper went down the stairs to look for lights or landmarks, found nothing and came back up. That would explain why he didn't answer the crew's first two calls before saying "everything is okay" at 8:05 110 / 254 / 551. His weight on the stairs should also have moved the cabin gauge the crew was watching, though, and they reported nothing until 8:10. So this fits the missed calls better than it fits Kinsler's "bump."
  • Search coverage. The 1972 Army search covered about 20–25 sq mi of dense timber, and the 1973 search was a single square mile. A right zone isn't the same as a thoroughly searched one.
Tena Bar (a later installment)

In February 1980, $5,800 of the ransom turned up on the Columbia River shore at Tena Bar, about 15 miles south-southwest of Point A 43 / 377 / 17613. Water doesn't explain it easily: the Lewis River, which drains the zone, joins the Columbia downstream of Tena Bar. That's why the FBI widened its zone to Clark County in 1980. It's an open question for the zone as a whole, not something the jump-time or wind analysis settles, and it gets its own report next.

Bottom line

The FBI/NWA January 1972 zone is the best-supported estimate of where Cooper came down, assuming he left the plane at 8:11 ±1 minute, which every record made at the time supports. The main later challenges to it, a "later jump" and a 65-knot southerly wind, don't survive the records or the 1971 weather data.

Appendix B

Data and method notes

  • True airspeed from indicated airspeed with standard-atmosphere pressure altitude and the sounding temperature: 170 KIAS at 10,000 ft and −12°C ≈ 195 kt TAS.
  • Wind from timing: headwind = average true airspeed − ground speed per leg. The 7:43–8:11 leg mixes 7 minutes at 7,000 ft/160 KIAS, a 3-minute climb and 18 minutes at 10,000 ft/170–180 KIAS.
  • Georeferencing: least-squares fits of FBI map scans to landmark coordinates. Residuals about 0.1–0.65 km on the La Center quadrangle maps and 1–3 km on the 12/4/71 chart.
  • Model runs and data files are kept with the research folder (drift model, wind check, sounding extracts, KML layers), so the numbers here can be rerun or challenged.
  • Magnetic variation about 21° east (Battle Ground VORTAC, 1975 listing). Pilot courses and tower winds are magnetic; weather-report and sounding winds are true.
